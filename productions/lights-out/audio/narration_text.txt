In my town, the power shuts off every night at exactly nine o'clock. Not one streetlight. Not one TV. Total darkness. So every kid here learns to walk home in the dark by counting steps. Most kids only count the big ones. Three hundred to the bakery. Six hundred to school. But I count everything. Stairs. Fence posts. Cracks in the sidewalk. Out loud. That's why everyone at school calls me Calculator. Only one light is allowed to stay on after nine: the lighthouse on the rocks. It has its own generator, and my grandpa has kept it shining for forty years. But grandpa's eyes are getting worse. Now he holds the rail with both hands. So every evening, I climb with him. Two hundred and fourteen steps to the top. I've counted them a thousand times. Up there, he showed me an old oil lamp. "If the generator ever dies," he said, "this is what keeps the boats safe." And when I counted, grandpa just smiled. "Lighthouse keepers count too." Then came the night of the big storm. My dad's fishing boat was still out. He's always home before nine. That night, he wasn't. At nine o'clock, the town went black, like always. Then, out on the rocks, the lighthouse flickered… and went out. The generator was dead. And somewhere in that storm, my dad was heading straight for the rocks, with nothing to guide him. Grandpa grabbed his coat, but he couldn't even find the door. "Leo," he whispered. "I can't do the stairs." So I went. Six hundred and twelve steps from our door to the lighthouse. I counted every one in the rain. Inside, it was so dark I couldn't see my own hands. But I didn't need to see. Seventy-one, the cracked step. One hundred and fifty, the turn. Two hundred and fourteen. I found the matches exactly where grandpa keeps them. Third shelf. Left side. The first match blew out. So did the second. The third one caught. The old lamp roared to life, and the giant lens threw its light across the sea. Far out in the waves, a tiny boat turned away from the rocks. Just in time. Dad's boat reached the harbor at ten fifty-two. I know, because I counted every minute. The whole town was waiting on the dock in the dark, and grandpa hugged me like he'd never let go. Now, on stormy nights, nobody laughs at the kid who counts. They count with me.
